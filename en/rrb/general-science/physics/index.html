Deep Dive

I. The Foundation of Physics: Measurement

Physics is often called the "science of measurement" because every theory must be verified through experiment and quantification. A Physical Quantity is any characteristic of matter or energy that can be measured (e.g., length, mass, time). To measure such a quantity, we compare it against a standard reference called a Unit.

  • Fundamental Quantities: These are independent quantities that typically cannot be defined in terms of other quantities. There are only seven in the SI system: Length, Mass, Time, Electric Current, Thermodynamic Temperature, Amount of Substance, and Luminous Intensity.

  • Derived Quantities: All other physical quantities are derived from the fundamental ones. For example, Speed is Length divided by Time (L/T), and Force is Mass times Acceleration (M · L/T2).

II. Systems of Units

Before the global standardization, different regions used different systems, leading to confusion in scientific communication.

  • CGS System (French): Uses Centimetre (Length), Gram (Mass), and Second (Time). Force unit is Dyne; Energy unit is Erg.

  • FPS System (British): Uses Foot, Pound, and Second. It is still used in everyday US life but largely abandoned in science.

  • MKS System (Metric): Uses Metre, Kilogram, and Second. This evolved into the modern SI system.

  • SI System (Système International): Adopted in 1960. It standardized the 7 fundamental units and 2 supplementary units (Radian for plane angle, Steradian for solid angle).

III. Scalar vs. Vector Quantities

Understanding the directionality of quantities is crucial in mechanics.

  • Scalar Quantities: Defined by magnitude only. They follow simple algebraic addition.

  • Examples: Mass (5 kg), Temperature (30°C), Time (10 s), Distance, Speed, Work, Power, Energy, Pressure, Volume, Density.

  • Vector Quantities: Defined by both magnitude and direction. They follow the Triangle Law or Parallelogram Law of vector addition.

  • Examples: Displacement (5 km North), Velocity, Acceleration, Force, Momentum, Torque, Electric Field.

IV. Dimensions and Dimensional Formulas

A dimensional formula expresses a derived quantity in terms of fundamental units (M, L, T). It is written in square brackets.

  • Velocity: Displacement/Time = L1 T-1 = [M0 L1 T-1].

  • Acceleration: Velocity/Time = L T-2 = [M0 L1 T-2].

  • Force: Mass × Acceleration = M · L T-2 = [M1 L1 T-2].

  • Work/Energy: Force × Displacement = M L T-2 · L = [M1 L2 T-2].

  • Importance: Dimensional analysis checks the correctness of physical equations (Principle of Homogeneity) and helps convert units between systems (e.g., 1 Joule = 107 Ergs).

V. Significant Astronomical Units

Measuring the universe requires units far larger than the metre.

  • Astronomical Unit (AU): The mean distance between the Earth and the Sun. 1 AU ≈ 1.496 × 1011 m. Used for distances within the solar system.

  • Light Year (ly): The distance light travels in a vacuum in one year. Speed of light (c) = 3 × 108 m/s. Therefore, 1 ly ≈ 9.46 × 1015 m. It is a unit of distance, not time.

  • Parsec (Parallax Second): The largest practical unit of distance used in astronomy. It is defined based on the parallax angle of a star. 1 Parsec ≈ 3.26 Light Years ≈ 3.08 × 1016 m.

Motion & Force

Newton's Laws of Motion

  • • 1st Law (Inertia): Object remains at rest/motion unless acted upon by force. (Galileo's Law).

  • • 2nd Law (Force): F = ma. Rate of change of momentum is proportional to force.

  • • 3rd Law (Action-Reaction): For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. (Rocket launch, Walking).

Work, Energy & Power

  • • Kinetic Energy: KE = ½ mv².

  • • Potential Energy: PE = mgh.

  • • Power: Work done / Time. (1 Horsepower = 746 Watts).

  • • Gravitation: g = 9.8 m/s² (Earth surface). g is 1/6th on Moon. Escape Velocity = 11.2 km/s.

Deep Dive

I. Fundamentals of Motion (Kinematics)

Kinematics is the study of motion without considering its cause (force).

  • Distance vs. Displacement: Distance is the total path length (Scalar). Displacement is the shortest straight line from start to finish (Vector). Displacement can be zero even if distance is not (e.g., one complete circular lap).

  • Speed vs. Velocity: Speed is Distance/Time (Scalar). Velocity is Displacement/Time (Vector). Velocity changes if speed changes OR direction changes.

  • Acceleration: The rate of change of velocity (a = Δv / t). Measured in m/s2. Negative acceleration is called retardation or deceleration.

  • Equations of Motion (Uniformly Accelerated):

  • v = u + at

  • s = ut + 1/2 at2

  • v2 = u2 + 2as (where u=initial velocity, v=final velocity, s=displacement).

II. Newton's Three Laws of Motion (Dynamics)

Dynamics considers the forces causing motion. Sir Isaac Newton formulated these laws in 1687.

  • First Law (Law of Inertia): An object stays at rest or in uniform motion unless acted upon by an external unbalanced force. Inertia is the property of matter to resist change. Mass is the measure of inertia (heavier object = more inertia). Examples: Passengers fall backward when a bus starts suddenly (Inertia of Rest).

  • Second Law (Law of Momentum): The rate of change of momentum is directly proportional to applied force (F ∝ dp/dt). This leads to F = ma. It defines Force. Momentum (p = mv) is the "quantity of motion". A cricket fielder pulls hands back to increase time (dt) and reduce force (F) of impact.

  • Third Law (Action-Reaction): For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. Characteristics: Action and Reaction act on two different bodies simultaneously. Examples: Recoil of a gun, Swimming (pushing water back moves swimmer forward), Rocket propulsion.

III. Force, Friction, and Circular Motion

  • Friction: A contact force opposing relative motion. Static friction (self-adjusting) > Kinetic (sliding) friction > Rolling friction. Ball bearings convert sliding to rolling friction to reduce wear.

  • Centripetal Force: Required for circular motion. Acts towards the center (F = mv2/r). Real force (e.g., tension in string, gravity for planets).

  • Centrifugal Force: A pseudo-force felt in a rotating frame (acting outwards). Example: Cream separator, passengers pushed outward in a turning car.

IV. Work, Energy, and Power

  • Work (W): W = F · s · cosθ. Work is zero if displacement is zero (pushing a wall) or if force is perpendicular to displacement (coolie carrying load on head horizontally).

  • Energy: Capacity to do work.

  • Kinetic Energy (KE): Due to motion (KE = 1/2 mv2). Cannot be negative.

  • Potential Energy (PE): Due to position (PE = mgh). Stored energy (dam water, stretched spring).

  • Conservation of Energy: Energy transforms but total remains constant. (e.g., Falling object: PE decreases, KE increases).

  • Power (P): Rate of doing work (P = W/t). SI Unit: Watt (W). 1 Horsepower (hp) = 746 Watts.

V. Gravitation

Gravity is the weakest of the four fundamental forces but dominates at macroscopic scales.

  • Newton's Law: F = G (m1 · m2) / r2. G is the Universal Gravitational Constant (6.67 × 10-11 Nm2/kg2).

  • Acceleration due to Gravity (g): On Earth, g ≈ 9.8 m/s2. Values:

  • Maximum at Poles, Minimum at Equator (due to Earth's bulge).

  • Zero at the center of Earth.

  • Decreases with altitude (height) and depth.

  • Escape Velocity: Minimum velocity to escape Earth's gravity: 11.2 km/s. For Moon, it is only 2.4 km/s, which is why Moon has no atmosphere (gas molecules escape).

  • Weight vs. Mass: Mass (m) is constant everywhere. Weight (W = mg) varies with gravity. Person weight on Moon is 1/6th of Earth.

Deep Dive

I. Nature of Light

Light is a form of energy that produces the sensation of vision. It behaves as both a Wave (Electromagnetic Transverse wave) and a Particle (Photons).

  • Speed of Light: Highest in vacuum (c ≈ 3 × 108 m/s). It slows down in denser media (Glass < Water < Air < Vacuum).

  • Straight Line Propagation: Light travels in straight lines (rectilinear propagation), which causes the formation of shadows and eclipses.

II. Reflection of Light

The bouncing back of light into the same medium when it strikes a surface.

  • Plane Mirror: Forms a virtual, erect, and same-sized image. Object distance = Image distance. Shows Lateral Inversion (Left appears Right). Used in Periscopes.

  • Spherical Mirrors:

  • Concave Mirror (Converging): Can form Real (inverted) or Virtual (erect) images. Focus matches the sun's rays. Uses: Torches, Headlights (reflector), Solar Cookers, Shaving mirrors (magnified image when close), Dentists.

  • Convex Mirror (Diverging): Always forms a virtual, erect, and diminished image. Offers a wide field of view. Uses: Rear-view mirrors in vehicles, Shop security mirrors.

III. Refraction of Light

The bending of light when it passes obliquely from one transparent medium to another due to a change in speed.

  • Principles:

  • Rarer to Denser (Air to Water): Bends Towards Normal.

  • Denser to Rarer (Water to Air): Bends Away from Normal.

  • Examples: Pencil appearing bent in water; Coin appearing raised in a cup; Twinkling of stars (Atmospheric Refraction); Early sunrise and delayed sunset (by 2 mins each).

  • Total Internal Reflection (TIR): When light goes from Denser to Rarer and the incident angle > Critical Angle, light reflects entirely back. Applications: Optical Fibres (communication with zero loss), Sparkling of Diamonds, Mirage (illusion of water on hot roads).

IV. Lenses and Human Eye

  • Convex Lens: Converging. Used in Microscopes, Telescopes, Cameras. Forms real images.

  • Concave Lens: Diverging. Forms virtual, smaller images.

  • Power of Lens: P = 1/f (in meters). Unit: Diopter (D). Convex (+D), Concave (-D).

  • Human Eye Defects:

  • Myopia (Near-sightedness): Can see near, not far. Image forms before retina. Correction: Concave Lens.

  • Hypermetropia (Far-sightedness): Can see far, not near. Image forms behind retina. Correction: Convex Lens.

  • Presbyopia: Old age sight. Weak ciliary muscles. Correction: Bifocal Lens.

  • Astigmatism: Blurry vision at all distances. Correction: Cylindrical Lens.

V. Dispersion and Scattering

  • Dispersion: Splitting of white light into 7 colors (VIBGYOR) by a prism. Violet bends most (shortest wavelength), Red bends least (longest wavelength). Rainbow: Caused by dispersion, refraction, and TIR in water droplets.

  • Scattering (Rayleigh): Scattering ∝ 1/λ4. Small particles scatter short wavelengths (Blue) more.

  • Blue Sky: Blue light scatters in all directions.

  • Red Sun at Sunrise/Sunset: Sunlight travels long distance; blue scatters away, leaving red (long wavelength) to reach eye.

  • Clouds are White: Large water droplets scatter all wavelengths equally (Mie scattering).

  • Space is Black: No atmosphere to scatter light.

Important Laws & Formulas

  • • Ohm's Law: V = IR (Voltage = Current × Resistance).

  • • Power: P = VI = I²R = V²/R.

  • • Resistance in Series: R = R1 + R2 (Current same).

  • • Resistance in Parallel: 1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2 (Voltage same).

  • • Heat (Joule's Law): H = I²Rt.

Household & Devices

  • • Fuse Wire: Made of Lead-Tin alloy (Pb-Sn). Low melting point. Series connection.

  • • Filament Bulb: Tungsten (High melting point). Filled with Nitrogen/Argon.

  • • Heater Coil: Nichrome (Ni + Cr). High resistance.

  • • Transformer: Works on Mutual Induction (AC only). Changes Voltage up/down.

Deep Dive

I. Electric Current and Potential

Electricity is the flow of charge.

  • Electric Charge (Q): Property of matter. Unit: Coulomb (C). Charge on 1 electron = 1.6 × 10-19 C.

  • Current (I): Rate of flow of charge (I = Q/t). Unit: Ampere (A). Measured by Ammeter (connected in series). Conventional current flows from Positive (+) to Negative (-), opposite to electron flow.

  • Potential Difference (V): Work done to move unit charge (V = W/Q). Unit: Volt (V). Battery provides "voltage" or "electric pressure" to push electrons. Measured by Voltmeter (connected in parallel).

II. Ohm's Law and Resistance

Provided temperature remains constant, V ∝ I, so V = IR.

  • Resistance (R): Opposition to current flow. Unit: Ohm (Ω).

  • Factors Affecting Resistance (R = ρ · L/A):

  • Length (L): Long wire = More resistance directly.

  • Area (A): Thick wire = Less resistance (inversely).

  • Material (ρ): Resistivity depends on material. Metals (Low ρ), Insulators (High ρ).

  • Temperature: Resistance of metals increases with temperature. Semi-conductors (Si, Ge) resistance decreases with temp.

III. Circuit Connections

  • Series Circuit: Same Current flows through all. Voltage divides. Req = R1 + R2. Drawback: If one appliance fails, the circuit breaks. Total resistance increases. Decorative lights are in series.

  • Parallel Circuit: Same Voltage across all. Current divides. 1/Req = 1/R1 + 1/R2. Benefit: Independent operation. Total resistance decreases. Household wiring is Parallel.

IV. Heating Effect and Power

  • Joule's Law of Heating: H = I2 R t. Heat produced depends on square of current, resistance, and time.

  • Applications:

  • Electric Bulb: Tungsten filament (High melting point ~3380°C + High resistance). Filled with Argon/Nitrogen to prevent oxidation.

  • Fuse: Safety device. Alloy of Lead & Tin (Pb-Sn). Low melting point, high resistance. Melts if current exceeds limit. Connected in Live wire (Series).

  • Heater/Iron: Nichrome element (Ni+Cr). Does not oxidize at high temp.

  • Electric Power: Rate of energy consumption. P = VI = I2R. Unit: Watt. Commercial Unit: kWh (Unit). 1 Unit = 1 kWh = 3.6 × 106 Joules.

V. Magnetism

  • Magnetic Field: Area where magnetic force is felt. Field lines emerge from North and enter South (outside magnet). Inside, they go South to North.

  • Electromagnetism (Oersted): Current carrying wire creates a magnetic field. Solenoid: Coil behaves like bar magnet. Used to make temporary magnets (Electromagnets) which are stronger than permanent magnets.

  • EMI (Faraday): Moving magnet near coil induces current. Principle of Generators (Mechanical to Electrical).

  • AC vs DC:

  • AC (Alternating Current): Direction changes periodically. Can travel long distances with low loss (via Transformers). Household supply (220V, 50Hz).

  • DC (Direct Current): Unidirectional. Batteries, Solar cells.

Deep Dive

I. Sound Waves

Sound is a mechanical wave produced by vibration.

  • Nature: Longitudinal wave (Particles vibrate parallel to propagation). Requires a medium (Solid, Liquid, Gas) — cannot travel in vacuum (Space). Consists of Compressions (High pressure) and Rarefactions (Low pressure).

  • Speed of Sound: Solids > Liquids > Gases. Speed of sound in Air ≈ 343 m/s (at 20°C). Steel ≈ 5960 m/s. Speed increases with Temperature (0.61 m/s per °C) and Humidity (moist air is lighter). Pressure has no effect on speed.

  • Frequency Range:

  • Infrasonic ( < 20 Hz): Generated by earthquakes, whales, elephants.

  • Audible Range (20 Hz - 20 kHz): Detectable by human ears.

  • Ultrasonic ( > 20 kHz): Bats, Dolphins, SONAR. Uses: Medical Imaging (Ultrasound), cleaning parts, detecting flaws in metal.

II. Properties of Sound

  • Loudness: Determined by Amplitude. Measured in Decibels (dB). Above 80 dB is noise.

  • Pitch (Shrillness): Determined by Frequency. Women/Children have high pitch voice (High Freq). Lion's roar has low pitch.

  • Echo: Reflection of sound. Minimum distance required: 17.2 meters (Persistence of hearing is 0.1s). Reverberation is multiple reflections (reduced by sound-proofing).

  • SONAR: Sound Navigation And Ranging. Uses Ultrasonic waves to clear measure ocean depth or find sunken ships (d = v × t / 2).

  • Doppler Effect: Change in observed frequency when source/observer moves (e.g., passing train whistle sounds higher approaching, lower receding).

III. Heat and Temperature

Heat is a form of energy (Joule). Temperature is the degree of hotness (Kelvin/Celsius).

  • Thermometers: Mercury is used because it doesn't stick to glass, expands uniformly, and has a high boiling point. Alcohol thermometers used for very low temps.

  • Scales:

  • Celsius (°C): Water freezes at 0, boils at 100.

  • Fahrenheit (°F): Water freezes at 32, boils at 212.

  • Kelvin (K): SI Unit. K = C + 273.15. Absolute Zero (0 K) is where molecular motion stops.

  • Formula: C/5 = (F-32)/9.

IV. Transmission of Heat

  • Conduction: Heat transfer in solids without particle movement (vibration only). Metals are good conductors (Free electrons). Wood/Plastic are insulators.

  • Convection: Heat transfer in fluids (liquids/gases) by actual movement of heated particles. Hot air rises, cold air sinks (Sea Breeze, Boiling water).

  • Radiation: Transfer without medium (electromagnetic waves). Heat from Sun reaches Earth via radiation. Black bodies absorb and radiate heat best (why radiators are black, white clothes for summer).

V. Key Thermal Concepts

  • Specific Heat Capacity: Heat required to raise temp of unit mass by 1°C. Water has very high specific heat — it heats up slow and cools down slow. Used as coolant in car engines and for fomentation.

  • Latent Heat: Heat absorbed/released during phase change at constant temperature (Solid-Liquid, Liquid-Gas). Steam burns are worse than boiling water burns because steam has extra Latent Heat of Vaporization.

  • Anomalous Expansion of Water: Water is densest at 4°C. When cooled from 4°C to 0°C, it expands. Ice is less dense than water, so it floats. This insulates water below, saving aquatic life in winter.

  • Evaporation: Surface phenomenon causing cooling (sweating, earthen pot water). Increases with surface area, temp, wind; decreases with humidity.
