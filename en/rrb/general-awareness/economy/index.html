Deep Dive

Evolution of Planning in India

The concept of economic planning in India was inspired by the Soviet Union model (Gosplan). The planning era began in 1950 with the establishment of the Planning Commission under the chairmanship of Jawaharlal Nehru. The fundamental objective was to initiate a process of development that would raise living standards and open out to the people new opportunities for a richer and more varied life. The First Five Year Plan (1951-56) was based on the Harrod-Domar Model, which emphasized that investment is key to economic growth. It prioritized agriculture and irrigation to address the immediate food crisis following partition.

Industrialization and Structural Shifts

The Second Plan (1956-61) marked a paradigm shift towards rapid industrialization, specifically heavy industries like steel and power. Based on the P.C. Mahalanobis Model, it aimed to build a domestic industrial base to reduce import dependence. This led to the establishment of PSUs (Public Sector Undertakings) and steel plants in Bhilai, Rourkela, and Durgapur. However, the neglect of agriculture combined with external aggressions (wars with China in 1962 and Pakistan in 1965) derailed the Third Plan, necessitating a "Plan Holiday" (1966-69) where annual plans were implemented to stabilize the economy.

Growth with Social Justice

From the Fourth Plan onwards, the focus shifted to "Growth with Stability" and later "Garibi Hatao" (Poverty Alleviation) during the Fifth Plan. This era saw significant state intervention, including the nationalization of banks (1969 and 1980) to direct credit to rural areas. The "Green Revolution" during this period transformed India from a food-importing nation to a self-sufficient one. The reform period began with the Eighth Plan (1992-97) post the 1991 Balance of Payment crisis, ushering in Liberalization, Privatization, and Globalization (LPG).

Transition to NITI Aayog

The Twelfth Plan (2012-17) was the final five-year plan, focusing on "Faster, Sustainable, and More Inclusive Growth." In 2015, the government replaced the Planning Commission with NITI Aayog (National Institution for Transforming India). Unlike the top-down approach of the Planning Commission, NITI Aayog employs a "bottom-up" approach, fostering cooperative federalism where states are equal partners in policy-making. It acts as a think tank, providing strategic and technical advice rather than allocating funds.

Topic 2 Banking & RBI

Repo Rate: Rate at which RBI lends to banks (Short term).

Deep Dive

Monetary Policy Tools

  • Repo Rate: Rate at which RBI lends to banks (Short term).

  • Reverse Repo: Rate at which RBI borrows from banks.

  • CRR (Cash Reserve Ratio): Cash kept by banks with RBI (No interest).

  • SLR (Statutory Liquidity Ratio): Liquid assets (Gold/Cash) kept by banks with themselves.

  • Bank Rate: Long term lending rate (Penal rate).

Banking Structure:

  • SBI: Largest Public Sector Bank (Imperial Bank renamed in 1955).

  • NABARD: Agriculture & Rural Dev (Shivraman Committee, 1982).

  • SIDBI: Small Industries (HQ: Lucknow, 1990).

  • EXIM Bank: Export-Import (1982).

  • SEBI: Capital Market Regulator (1988/1992). HQ: Mumbai.

Role and Functions of RBI

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) is the apex monetary institution of India. Preamble of the RBI Act, 1934, describes its basic functions: to regulate the issue of Bank notes and keeping of reserves with a view to securing monetary stability in India and generally to operate the currency and credit system of the country to its advantage. It acts as the "Lender of Last Resort" to banks, manages the country's foreign exchange reserves under FEMA, and serves as the banker to the central and state governments.

Monetary Policy Framework

The primary objective of monetary policy is to maintain price stability while keeping in mind the objective of growth. In 2016, a flexible inflation targeting framework was adopted, where the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)—a 6-member body headed by the RBI Governor—decides the policy Repo Rate to keep inflation within a target band of 4% (+/- 2%).

  • Quantitative Tools: These directly affect the quantity of money (Liquidity).

Repo Rate: The rate at which RBI lends short-term money to banks. An increase in Repo Rate makes borrowing costly, reducing money supply and controlling inflation.

  • CRR (Cash Reserve Ratio): The share of Net Demand and Time Liabilities (NDTL) that banks must maintain as cash with the RBI. It earns no interest.

  • SLR (Statutory Liquidity Ratio): The share of NDTL banks must maintain in liquid assets like gold, cash, or approved securities.

  • Qualitative Tools: These direct credit to specific sectors. Examples include Margin Requirements, Moral Suasion, and Priority Sector Lending (PSL) targets.

Evolution of Banking Sector

The Indian banking sector has undergone massive transformation. The nationalization waves of 1969 (14 banks) and 1980 (6 banks) aimed to shift banking from "class banking" to "mass banking." Recent reforms include the merger of Public Sector Banks (reducing their count from 27 to 12) to create large, globally competitive banks. The Narasimham Committee recommendations led to the introduction of private players and modern prudential norms (Basel Norms) to ensure capital adequacy.

Financial Inclusion & New Age Banking

To reach the unbanked, RBI introduced niche banks like Payments Banks (e.g., Paytm, India Post) which cannot lend but can accept deposits, and Small Finance Banks (e.g., AU, Ujjivan) which focus on lending to small business units and unorganized sectors. The "JAM Trinity" (Jan Dhan - Aadhaar - Mobile) has been instrumental in direct benefit transfers (DBT), reducing leakages and ensuring subsidies reach the intended beneficiaries.

Topic 3 Important Government Schemes

Government schemes in India act as the primary vehicle for socio-economic development. In recent years, there has been a noticeable shift from pure subsidy-based welfare to schemes that empower citizens through asset creation, skill development, and financial inclusion.

Deep Dive

Welfare to Empowerment

Government schemes in India act as the primary vehicle for socio-economic development. In recent years, there has been a noticeable shift from pure subsidy-based welfare to schemes that empower citizens through asset creation, skill development, and financial inclusion.

Agriculture & Rural Development

PM-KISAN (Pradhan Mantri Kisan Samman Nidhi) is a central sector scheme that provides income support of ₹6,000 per year to all landholding farmer families. This amount is released in three installments directly into bank accounts, bypassing middlemen. Complementing this is the Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana (PMFBY), which offers comprehensive crop insurance against non-preventable natural risks at very low premiums (2% for Kharif, 1.5% for Rabi).

MNREGA (Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act) is unique as it guarantees the "Right to Work" legally. It promises at least 100 days of wage employment in a financial year to every rural household whose adult members volunteer to do unskilled manual work. It serves as a critical safety net during agricultural lean seasons and distress.

Health & Sanitation

Ayushman Bharat (PM-JAY) is the world's largest government-funded healthcare program. It provides a health cover of ₹5 lakh per family per year for secondary and tertiary care hospitalization to over 12 crore poor and vulnerable families. The scheme is completely cashless and paperless at public and empaneled private hospitals.

The Swachh Bharat Abhiyan (SBM), launched in 2014, triggered a behavioral change regarding sanitation. Phase 2 (SBM-G 2.0) now focuses on sustaining the Open Defecation Free (ODF) status and managing solid and liquid waste (ODF Plus).

Financial Security & Housing

The Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana (PMAY) aims to provide "Housing for All." PMAY-Gramin and PMAY-Urban provide assistance to construct pucca houses with basic amenities like toilet, water, and electricity connection. Under PM Jan Dhan Yojana, over 50 crore bank accounts have been opened, bringing the unbanked into the formal financial system, enabling access to credit, insurance, and pension schemes like Atal Pension Yojana.

Topic 4 High Yield Topics

Amendment: 101st Constitutional Amendment Act.

Deep Dive

GST (Goods and Services Tax)

  • Amendment: 101st Constitutional Amendment Act.

  • Article: 246A (Power to tax), 279A (GST Council).

  • Type: Destination based Indirect Tax.

  • Slabs: 5%, 12%, 18%, 28% (0% for essentials).

  • First Country: France (1954). First State: Assam.

Economic Concepts & Terms

GDP: Total value of goods/services produced within country boundary. GNP: GDP + Net income from abroad. Per Capita Income: National Income / Total Population. Inflation: General rise in price levels (Value of money falls). Deflation: Fall in price levels. Fiscal Deficit: Total Exp - Total Income (excluding borrowing).

Budget and Fiscal Policy

The Union Budget (Article 112) is the annual financial statement of the government. It has two main parts: the Revenue Budget (recurring income/expenditure like taxes and salaries) and the Capital Budget (assets/liabilities like infrastructure spending and loans). A key indicator of fiscal health is the Fiscal Deficit, which represents the total borrowing requirements of the government. The FRBM Act (Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management) sets targets to keep this deficit in check to prevent inflation and debt accumulation.

The NITI Aayog Era

Established on January 1, 2015, NITI Aayog (National Institution for Transforming India) serves as the government's premier policy think tank. Unlike the Planning Commission, it does not have the power to allocate funds (which is now done by the Finance Ministry). Its structure involves the Prime Minister as Chairperson and Chief Ministers of all states in the Governing Council, promoting Cooperative Federalism. It focuses on "Outcome-based monitoring" and fostering competition among states through indices like the School Education Quality Index (SEQI) and Health Index.

GST: One Nation, One Tax

The Goods and Services Tax (GST), implemented on July 1, 2017, via the 101st Constitutional Amendment, revolutionized India's indirect taxation. It subsumed multiple central and state taxes like VAT, Service Tax, and Excise Duty into a single unified tax.

  • GST Council (Article 279A): The decision-making body chaired by the Union Finance Minister, with State Finance Ministers as members. It decides tax rates and exemptions.

  • Structure: It has a 4-tier slab structure (5%, 12%, 18%, 28%). Key items like alcohol, petrol, and electricity are currently outside GST.

  • Impact: It eliminated the "Cascading Effect" (tax on tax), reduced logistics costs, and expanded the tax base, fostering a unified national market.

Topic 5 International Organizations

Deep Dive

  • IMF (International Monetary Fund): Bretton Woods Twin.

Geneva, Switzerland

  • WTO: World Trade Org (Est 1995, replaced GATT).

  • WHO: World Health Org (Apr 7, 1948).

  • ILO: International Labour Org.

Others

  • UNESCO: Paris, France.

  • UN Headquarters: New York, USA.

  • ADB (Asian Development Bank): Manila, Philippines.

  • SAARC: Kathmandu, Nepal.

  • ASEAN: Jakarta, Indonesia.

Global Economic Architecture

International organizations play a pivotal role in maintaining global economic stability, trade, and development. The architecture was largely shaped after World War II with the Bretton Woods Conference (1944), which birthed the "Bretton Woods Twins"—the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund (IMF).

World Bank vs. IMF

While both are HQ'd in Washington D.C., their mandates differ. The IMF focuses on global monetary stability and provides short-term loans to countries facing Balance of Payments (BoP) crises (like India in 1991). It monitors exchange rates and economic health. The World Bank, primarily through its arm IBRD (International Bank for Reconstruction and Development), focuses on long-term development. It provides loans and grants to developing countries for infrastructure, poverty reduction, health, and education projects.

World Trade Organization (WTO)

Established in 1995 (successor to GATT), the WTO rules global trade. Headquartered in Geneva, it ensures trade flows as smoothly, predictably, and freely as possible. Its diverse functions include administering trade agreements, handling trade disputes (e.g., between US and India), and providing technical assistance. India is a founding member and actively advocates for the rights of developing nations, especially in agriculture (subsidies) and services.

Emerging Blocs: BRICS & G20

The global economic order is shifting towards multi-polarity. BRICS (Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa) established the New Development Bank (NDB) in Shanghai as an alternative to western-dominated institutions. Similarly, the G20, a forum of the world's 20 largest economies, has become the premier forum for international economic cooperation, addressing issues like global tax reforms and climate finance. India's G20 Presidency in 2023 highlighted the theme "Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam" (One Earth, One Family, One Future).
