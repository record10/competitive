Point Formulas

  • Distance: √[(x₂-x₁)² + (y₂-y₁)²]

  • Midpoint: [(x₁+x₂)/2, (y₁+y₂)/2]

  • Section (m:n): [(mx₂+nx₁)/(m+n), ...]

  • Centroid: [(x₁+x₂+x₃)/3, ...]

Line & Area

  • Slope (m): (y₂-y₁) / (x₂-x₁) = tan θ

  • Eqn: y = mx + c

  • Area of Triangle: ½ |x₁(y₂-y₃) + ...|

  • Collinear: Area = 0.

Deep Dive

The Geometry of Algebra Coordinate geometry, or analytic geometry, bridges the gap between algebra and geometry. It allows us to describe geometric shapes using algebraic equations. The position of any point is defined by a pair of numbers (x, y), representing its horizontal and vertical distance from the origin.

The Section Formula is a versatile tool. It finds the coordinates of a point dividing a line segment in a given ratio (m:n). This isn't just for internal division; it can also be used for external division by treating the ratio as negative. The Centroid of a triangle is its geometric center (center of gravity) and is simply the average of the coordinates of its vertices.

A key concept is determining if three points are collinear (lie on the same straight line). While you can check slopes, the most robust method for competitive exams is checking if the Area of the Triangle formed by these points is zero. The area formula, often involving determinant logic, simplifies quickly when numbers are plugged in.

Formula (n sides)

  • Sum of Interior Angles: (n - 2) × 180°

  • Each Interior Angle (Regular): [(n - 2) × 180°] / n

  • Sum of Exterior Angles: 360° (Always)

  • Each Exterior Angle (Regular): 360° / n

  • Number of Diagonals: n(n - 3) / 2

Deep Dive

The Power of Exterior Angles A polygon is a closed figure formed by line segments. Polygons are classified as "regular" if all their sides and angles are equal. A surprisingly powerful property of any convex polygon, regardless of the number of sides, is that the sum of its exterior angles is always 360°. This fact is often the quickest way to solve problems involving regular polygons. For instance, if you need to find the number of sides of a regular polygon with an exterior angle of 45°, you simply calculate 360/45 = 8 sides (Octagon).

The formula for the sum of interior angles, (n-2) × 180°, comes from the fact that any n-sided polygon can be divided into (n-2) triangles, and the sum of angles in a triangle is 180°.

Finding the number of diagonals using n(n-3)/2 is another frequent exam question. A diagonal connects two non-adjacent vertices. From each of the 'n' vertices, you can draw (n-3) diagonals (you can't connect to itself or its two neighbors). Multiplying n by (n-3) counts each diagonal twice (once from each end), hence the division by 2.

Similar Triangles (~)

  • Sides proportional: AB/PQ = BC/QR = AC/PR.

  • Area Ratio = (Side₁/Side₂)² = (Alt₁/Alt₂)².

  • BPT: Parallel line divides sides in same ratio.

Centers

  • Centroid (G): Median intersection (2:1).

  • Incenter (I): Angle bisector intersection.

  • Circumcenter (O): Perpendicular bisector.

  • Orthocenter (H): Altitude intersection.

Deep Dive

Similarity vs. Congruency Congruency means two triangles are identical in shape and size (they overlap perfectly). Similarity means they have the same shape but different sizes (one is a scaled version of the other). In similar triangles, corresponding angles are equal, and determining the ratio of corresponding sides allows you to find unknown lengths easily.

The ratio of Areas of two similar triangles is equal to the square of the ratio of their corresponding sides (or altitudes, or medians). This is a critical theorem. If the side ratio is 2:3, the area ratio is 4:9.

Understanding the centers of a triangle is vital. The Centroid divides every median in a 2:1 ratio. The Incenter is equidistant from all sides (center of the incircle). The Circumcenter is equidistant from all vertices (center of the circumcircle). The Orthocenter is the meeting point of altitudes. In an equilateral triangle, all these points coincide.

Circle Tangents & Chords

Tangents

  • From external point are equal (PA = PB).

  • PA × PB = PC × PD (Secants intersection).

  • PT² = PA × PB (Tangent-Secant).

  • Alternate Segment: Angle between tan/chord = Angle in alt segment.

Common Tangents

  • DCT: √[d² - (r1-r2)²]

  • TCT: √[d² - (r1+r2)²]

Deep Dive

The Power of Point P Circles are symmetric, and this symmetry gives rise to elegant theorems. A tangent touches the circle at exactly one point and is always perpendicular to the radius at that point. A key property to remember is that tangents drawn from an external point to a circle are equal in length. This forms congruent triangles and is the basis for many proofs involving inscribed circles.

The Tangent-Secant Theorem (PT² = PA × PB) establishes a relationship between the length of a tangent segment and a secant segment from the same external point.

The Alternate Segment Theorem is a bit tricky but very useful: The angle between a tangent and a chord through the point of contact is equal to the angle subtended by the chord in the alternate segment. Mastering this allows you to solve complex angle-chasing problems in seconds.
