Logical Reasoning

Syllogism (Statement & Conclusion)

Types of Statements

  • • All A are B: A is fully contained inside B.

  • • Some A are B: A intersects B (Common part exists).

  • • No A is B: A and B are disjoint (No contact).

  • • Some A are not B: Part of A is strictly outside B.

Key Rules

  • • Possibility: If no definite negative relation exists, possibility is generally True.

  • • Either-Or Case:

  • Both conclusions must be individually False.

  • Subject & Predicate must be the same.

  • Complimentary Pair (Some + No, All + Some Not).

Deep Dive

Syllogism is a form of deductive reasoning where you arrive at a specific conclusion by examining two or more premises (statements). The fundamental rule of Syllogism is to accept the given statements as absolute truth, even if they defy real-world logic (e.g., "All dogs are cats"). Your task is to determine which conclusions logically follow from the statements alone.

The Venn Diagram Method

The most robust and error-proof way to solve Syllogisms is using Venn Diagrams. This involves drawing circles to represent sets of items.

  • Basic Diagram: This represents the "minimum overlap" scenario. It shows exactly what the statement says and nothing more.

  • Possible Diagram: This represents all valid scenarios. A conclusion is considered True ONLY if it holds true in ALL possible diagrams (Basic + Possible). If it fails in even one diagram, it is False/Doubtful.

Decoding Complex Statements

Examiners often twist language to confuse candidates. Here are standard decodings:

  • "Only A is B": This is a reverse statement. It means "All B are A". Crucially, it implies a restriction: B cannot touch any other circle unless specified. For example, "Only A is B" means B is exclusively part of A.

  • "Only a few A are B": This is a dual statement. It combines "Some A are B" AND "Some A are NOT B". You must draw them intersecting, but also mark a part of A that distinctly does NOT touch B.

  • "At least/Mostly/Generally": These all translate to "Some". "At least some A are B" is mathematically identical to "Some A are B".

  • "Can be / Is a possibility": This checks for potential overlap. If there is no "No" (negative) restriction between two entities, their intersection is always a possibility. "Some A can be B" is True unless "No A is B" is given.

The "Either-Or" Condition

This is a critical concept for high accuracy. An "Either-Or" relationship exists when you cannot be sure about a specific relationship, but you know one of two alternatives must be true. Three conditions must be met:

  • Both conclusions must be False (Uncertain): Neither conclusion should be definitely true on its own.

  • Subject & Predicate must be the same: (e.g., Conclusion 1 talks about "Cats & Dogs", Conclusion 2 must also talk about "Cats & Dogs").

  • Complimentary Pair: The pair of conclusions must cover all possibilities.

  • Some + No: (e.g., "Some A are B" + "No A is B"). This is a valid pair.

  • All + Some Not: (e.g., "All A are B" + "Some A are not B"). This is a valid pair.

  • Important Exception: "All + No" is NOT an Either-Or pair because both can be false simultaneously (e.g., if "Some A are B" is true).

Deep Dive

Venn diagrams visualize logical relationships between sets. The primary skill tested here is the ability to translate real-world relationships into geometric configurations (usually circles). You must assess whether items belong to a group fully, partially, or not at all.

Mastering Set Relationships

There are 10 standard representations of Venn diagrams based on relationships:

  • Universal Subset (All-All-All): (e.g., India, Delhi, Red Fort). All Red Forts are in Delhi, all Delhi is in India. Represented by three concentric circles.

  • Two Disjoint Subsets in a Universal Set (All-No-No): (e.g., Furniture, Chair, Table). Chairs and Tables are separate items, but both are fully contained within the Furniture category. Represented by one big circle containing two smaller non-touching circles.

  • Partial Overlap (Some-Some-Some): (e.g., Teacher, Writer, Musician). A person can be a Teacher and a Writer; a Writer can be a Musician. They can potentially be all three. Represented by three intersecting circles.

  • Complex Relations: (e.g., Males, Fathers, Doctors). All Fathers are Males (All relation). Doctors can be Males or Fathers (Some relation). Represented by a "Father" circle inside "Male", intersected by "Doctor".

Data Interpretation in Venn

Often questions provide a geometric diagram (Triangles, Squares, Circles) with numbers in various regions. Each shape represents a category.

  • Union ($A cup B$): Total elements in A or B or Both. Formula: $n(A cup B) = n(A) + n(B) - n(A cap B)$.

  • Intersection ($A cap B$): The overlapping region (Both A and B). This is usually the key to solving the problem.

  • Difference ($A - B$): Elements in A but NOT in B (Only A).

Strategy for "Only" Regions

When specific numbers are given for "All three", "A and B", etc., always start filling the diagram from the innermost intersection (intersection of 3 sets) and work outwards.

Example: If the question says "10 people play BOTH Tennis and Cricket", and 2 of them also play Football, then:

  • "Only Tennis and Cricket" = 10 - 2 = 8.

  • "Tennis and Cricket" = 10 (Total intersection).

Always distinguish between "A and B" (total intersection) vs "Only A and B" (intersection minus the third set).

Deep Dive

The Geometry of Dice

A die is a cube with 6 faces, 8 corners (vertices), and 12 edges. It is a 3D object visualized in 2D.

  • Standard Dice: In a standard die, the numbers on opposite faces always add up to 7 (1 opposite 6, 2 opposite 5, 3 opposite 4). Consequently, the numbers on any two adjacent faces can never equal 7. If you see a die where 3 and 4 are adjacent, it is NOT a standard die.

  • General Dice: If any two adjacent faces add up to 7, it is a General Die. Here, standard rules don't apply, and relationships are determined by comparing two or more positions of the same die correctly.

Solving Techniques for Dice

When two or more positions of the same die are given, use these rules:

  • Rule of Rotation (One Common Face): If two positions show exactly one common number, write that number down. From there, move Clockwise in both figures and write the numbers in sequence. The numbers that align vertically are opposite to each other. The missing number is opposite the common number.

  • Rule of Elimination (Two Common Faces): If two positions have two numbers in common (e.g., a die shows 2, 3, 5 and another shows 2, 3, 6), then the third numbers (5 and 6) are definitely opposite each other. The opposites of the common numbers (2, 3) cannot be determined uniquely without more info.

Cutting Painted Cubes

If a large cube of side $N$ is painted on all sides (e.g., Red) and cut into smaller cubes of side $n$, then the number of parts per side is $x = N/n$. The total number of small cubes formed is $x^3$.

  • 3 Faces Painted: These are always the Corner Cubes. A cube has 8 corners, so the answer is always 8 (provided $x ge 2$).

  • 2 Faces Painted: These are the Edge Cubes (excluding corners). Formula: $12 imes (x - 2)$.

  • 1 Face Painted: These are the Face Cubes (excluding boundaries). Formula: $6 imes (x - 2)^2$.

  • 0 Faces Painted: These are the Inner Core Cubes (unseen). Formula: $(x - 2)^3$.

Visualization Tip: Imagine "peeling" the outer layer of the cube like an onion. The outer shell contains all the painted parts. What remains inside is the $(x-2)^3$ unpainted core.

Deep Dive

Non-verbal reasoning tests your ability to process visual information. Unlike verbal questions, there is no language barrier here, but the trap lies in subtle details. The primary method for solving these is Elimination. Instead of looking for the right figure, look for the wrong ones (e.g., wrong orientation, missing element) and eliminate them.

1. Mirror and Water Images

  • Mirror Image (Lateral Inversion): Imagine the object is flipping sideways. The Left side becomes Right, and Right becomes Left. Top and Bottom remain unchanged.

  • Symmetrical Letters: A, H, I, M, O, T, U, V, W, X, Y do not change in a vertical mirror.

  • Water Image (Vertical Inversion): Imagine the object is flipping upside down. Top becomes Bottom, and Bottom becomes Top. Left and Right remain unchanged.

  • Symmetrical Letters: C, D, E, H, I, K, O, X do not change in a water reflection.

2. Series and Analogy

In visual series, figures change step-by-step following a specific rule. You must identify the pattern.

  • Rotation: Elements rotate Clockwise (CW) or Anti-clockwise (ACW) by specific angles (45°, 90°, 135°).

  • Movement: Elements shift positions along the sides or diagonals of the frame. Checks for "skipping" steps (move 1 step, then 2 steps...).

  • Addition/Deletion: New elements (symbols, lines) are added or existing ones removed in each step.

  • Flip/Inversion: Elements flip specifically (e.g., alternate inversion).

3. Paper Cutting and Folding

This involves spatial visualization. The key is to trace the path backwards. The last fold made is the first one to be opened. Every time you open a fold, the cut mark creates a Mirror Image across the fold line. Symmetrical patterns often emerge.

Trick: If paper is folded twice (quarters), a single hole punch usually results in 4 holes. If folded diagonally, it results in symmetric placement across the diagonal.

4. Embedded Figures

You must find a hidden shape within a complex diagram. The orientation (rotation) is usually preserved unless the question specifies "rotation is not allowed". Focus on unique features like sharp corners, parallel lines, or specific angles to locate the shape quickly.
