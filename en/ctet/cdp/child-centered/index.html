Child Centered Education & Intelligence

Child-Centered & Progressive Education

CTET

Child-Centered Education is a modern pedagogical approach that shifts the focus from the teacher and the curriculum to the learner. In this model, the child is viewed as an active participant constructs their own knowledge, rather than a passive recipient.

  • Concept: Education should revolve around the child's needs, interests, and aptitudes. It opposes the traditional "Teacher-Centered" approach where the teacher is the absolute authority.

  • John Dewey: The father of Progressive Education. He established "Lab Schools" and emphasized:

  • Learning by Doing: Experiential learning through hands-on activities.

  • Education as Life: School is a miniature society, not just preparation for future life.

  • Democratic Methods: Giving children voice and choice in the classroom.

  • Role of Teacher: Shifts from "dictator" to Facilitator, Guide, and Observer. The teacher provides the environment and resources (Scaffolding).

  • Key Features:

  • Flexible Curriculum: Adapted to individual differences.

  • Continuous Assessment: Evaluation is diagnostic and integrated with teaching, not just end-of-term.

  • Holistic Development: Focus on physical, social, emotional, and cognitive growth.

Keywords for Exam: Active engagement, Hands-on, Autonomy, Democratic, Flexible, Constructivism.

Deep Dive

Child-Centered Education shifts the focus from the teacher and the curriculum to the learner. In this approach, the child is seen as an active participant in their own learning, not a passive vessel to be filled with information.

1. John Dewey's Progressive Education

John Dewey, an American philosopher, is the primary figure behind this movement. He argued that education should not be a "preparation for life" but "life itself."

  • Learning by Doing: Children learn best when they interact with their environment. Hands-on activities, experiments, and field trips are superior to rote memorization.
  • Democratic Classroom: The classroom should mirror a democratic society where children have a voice in what and how they learn. This fosters social responsibility.
  • Problem Solving: Education should present real-world problems that challenge children to think critically and collaboratively.

2. Role of the Teacher

In a child-centered classroom, the teacher is not an authoritarian "Sage on the Stage" but a "Guide on the Side."

  • Facilitator: The teacher provides the resources and environment necessary for learning. They set up the classroom to encourage exploration and ensure materials are accessible.
  • Observer: They actively observe each child's interests, strengths, and learning styles to tailor instruction (Differentiated Instruction). Assessment is continuous and diagnostic, not just end-of-term testing.
  • Partner: The relationship is one of mutual respect. The teacher learns from the students just as the students learn from the teacher. They model lifelong learning and curiosity.

3. Key Principles

  • Freedom and Autonomy: Children are given the freedom to choose activities, fostering a sense of responsibility.
  • Holistic Development: Focuses on the physical, emotional, social, and intellectual growth of the child, not just academic scores.
  • Cooperative Learning: Group work and peer learning are emphasized over competition. Children learn social skills by working together.

3. Comparison with Teacher-Centered

Teacher-Centered

  • Passive Learners: Students listen and take notes.

  • Rote Memorization: Focus on recalling facts accurately.

  • Discipline through fear: Silence is mandatory.

  • Uniform curriculum: One size fits all approach.

Child-Centered

  • Active Exploration: Students manipulate objects and discuss.

  • Critical Thinking: Focus on understanding 'Why' and 'How'.

  • Self-discipline: Order comes from engagement.

  • Flexible curriculum: Adapts to student needs.

CTET Note: Look for keywords like "Hands-on," "Active Engagement," "Democratic," and "Flexibility." Any option suggesting "Rote memorization," "Strict discipline," or "Standardized curriculum" is usually incorrect in this context.

Concept of Intelligence & Multi-Dimensional Intelligence

CTET

Intelligence is a complex, multi-dimensional construct referring to the ability to adjust to new environments, think abstractly, and learn from experience. It is NOT a single, fixed trait.

  • Changing Views:

  • Uni-factor Theory (Alfred Binet): Old view. Intelligence is one general ability (g-factor). Criticized for being too simplistic.

  • Multi-dimensional: Modern view. Set of distinct capabilities.

  • Howard Gardner's Multiple Intelligences (8 Types):

  • Linguistic: Language skill (Poet, Lawyer).

  • Logical-Mathematical: Number/Reasoning skill (Scientist, Coder).

  • Spatial: Visualizing 3D space (Architect, Pilot).

  • Bodily-Kinesthetic: Body movement (Dancer, Athlete).

  • Musical: Rhythm/Pitch (Musician).

  • Interpersonal: Understanding others (Teacher, Politician).

  • Intrapersonal: Understanding self (Philosopher).

  • Naturalistic: Nature/Classification (Farmer, Botanist).

  • Robert Sternberg's Triarchic Theory (PCA):

  • Practical (Contextual/Street Smart).

  • Creative (Experiential/Novelty).

  • Analytical (Componential/Book Smart).

  • IQ Formula: IQ = (Mental Age / Chronological Age) × 100. (Given by William Stern, refined by Terman).

Deep Dive

Intelligence was historically viewed as a single, general ability (g-factor) that determined a person's success. Modern theories, however, view it as multi-dimensional. Intelligence is the ability to adjust to the environment, learn from experience, and think abstractly.

1. Howard Gardner's Multiple Intelligences

Gardner argued that human beings have different ways of processing information and that these are relatively independent of one another. He proposed 8 distinctive intelligences (NCERT):

  • Linguistic: Skill with words and language (Poets, Writers, Lawyers).
  • Logical-Mathematical: Skill with numbers and logic (Scientists, Accountants).
  • Spatial: Ability to visualize the world in 3D (Architects, Pilots, Artists).
  • Musical: Sensitivity to rhythm and sound (Musicians, Composers).
  • Bodily-Kinesthetic: Skill in using the body (Athletes, Dancers, Surgeons).
  • Interpersonal: Understanding others' emotions and motivations (Politicians, Teachers, Social Workers).
  • Intrapersonal: Understanding oneself and one's own weakness/strengths (Philosophers, Gurus).
  • Naturalistic: Understanding nature and classification (Farmers, Botanists).

Trick: Inter = Enter others (Social). Intra = Inside self (Self).

2. Robert Sternberg's Triarchic Theory

Sternberg proposed that intelligence is comprised of three parts (Triarchic):

  • Practical Intelligence (Contextual): Street smarts. Ability to adapt to the environment and solve daily life problems.
  • Creative Intelligence (Experiential): Ability to deal with novel situations and create new ideas.
  • Analytical Intelligence (Componential): Book smarts. Ability to analyze, evaluate, and compare (Academic problem solving).

Trick: PCA (Practical, Creative, Analytical).

3. Alfred Binet (Uni-factor Theory)

Binet, the father of intelligence testing, believed typically in a 'General Intelligence' that affects performance on all cognitive tasks. This is considered too simplistic today.

4. IQ (Intelligence Quotient)

German psychologist William Stern coined the term IQ. Terman refined the formula:

IQ = (Mental Age / Chronological Age) × 100 Though useful, IQ tests are often criticized for being culturally biased. An average IQ is considered 90-109. Gifted children have IQ > 140.
