Socialization Process

Socialization Process

1. Primary Socialization

CTET

Primary Socialization is the foundational phase of learning that takes place in Infancy and Early Childhood. It is "Primary" because it happens first and shapes the core personality.

  • Main Agent: The Immediate Family (Parents, Siblings). The mother is often considered the first teacher.

  • Key Learnings:

  • Language Acquisition: Mother tongue is learned naturally.

  • Basic Trust: Emotional bonds and security (Attachment).

  • Norms: Basic hygiene (toilet training), eating etiquette, and gender roles.

  • Mechanism: It is informal, emotional, and particularistic (child is special). It involves Internalization—making society's rules one's own conscience.

Crucial Concept: It transforms a "Biological Being" into a "Social Being".

Deep Dive

Primary socialization is the foundation of all future social development. It occurs during the most critical years of life (Infancy and Early Childhood) within the immediate family. It is here that the biological being is transformed into a social being.

1. The Critical Role of Family

The family is the first agency of socialization. It is often called a "Micro-system" in Bronfenbrenner's theory. Through parents (especially the mother), the child learns:

  • Language: The "Mother Tongue" is acquired here without formal instruction.
  • Basic Habits: Hygiene (toilet training), eating manners, and dressing.
  • Emotional Bonds: Trust vs. Mistrust (Erikson’s first stage) is established here. A secure attachment leads to confidence; insecure attachment leads to anxiety.
  • Values & Norms: Differentiating between what is "Good" and "Bad" based on parental approval/disapproval.

2. Mechanism: Internalization

Talcott Parsons argued that the primary function of the family is the "production of personality." The child doesn't just learn rules; they internalize them. The rules of the family become the voice of the child's conscience (Freud’s Superego).

For example, a child doesn't just say "Thank you" because they are forced to, but because they start believing that gratitude is good. This internalization makes society possible because people follow rules even when no police are watching.

3. Formation of the "Self"

George Herbert Mead explained that children develop a "Self" by imitating "Significant Others" (mom/dad). In the "Play Stage," they role-play as parents (playing 'house'). This helps them understand the perspective of others, moving away from pure egocentrism.

CTET Exam Tip: Questions often ask: "Which is the primary agent of socialization?" The answer is always Family (or Immediate Family). School is a Secondary agent. Even if the option says "Family and Neighbourhood," choose it over "School and Media."

2. Secondary Socialization

CTET

Secondary Socialization occurs from Late Childhood onwards and continues throughout life. It marks the transition from the private world of home to the public world.

  • Main Agents:

  • School: First formal agency. Teaches rules, discipline, and academic skills.

  • Peer Group: Agency of equality. Teaches cooperation and negotiation.

  • Media: Passive agent. Influences attitudes and stereotypes.

  • Characteristics:

  • Formal interactions: Based on universalistic standards (rules apply to all).

  • Skill Learning: Vocational skills and citizenship duties.

Shift in Focus: Moving from "Particularistic" (I am special to mom) to "Universalistic" (I am just one student in class).

Deep Dive

Secondary socialization marks the transition from the "Micro-world" of the family to the "Macro-world" of society. It begins when the child enters formal institutions like school. The child learns to behave as a member of a larger community, not just a son or daughter.

1. School: The First Formal Agency

School is often the first place where children are judged by universalistic standards (same rules for everyone) rather than particularistic standards (parents treating their child as special). This introduces:

  • The Hidden Curriculum: Unwritten rules of behavior. Raising hands to speak, waiting in line, and respecting authority figures other than parents.
  • Social Heterogeneity: Interacting with children from different castes, religions, and economic backgrounds. This reduces egocentrism.
  • Achievement & Competition: Learning that rewards are based on performance (meritocracy).

2. Peer Groups: The Agency of Equality

As children grow (especially in Late Childhood and Adolescence), the peer group creates a sub-culture. It is unique because it is an Egalitarian relationship (power is shared equally), unlike the hierarchy of family/school.

  • Democratic Skills: Negotiation, compromise, and leadership are learned here.
  • Peer Pressure: Can be positive (study groups/sports) or negative (bullying/risky behavior).

3. Mass Media: The Passive Powerful Agent

In the 21st century, media (TV, YouTube, Social Media) rivals the family in influence.

  • Modeling Behavior: Children imitate what they watch (Bandura’s Social Learning Theory). Violent cartoons can increase aggression; educational shows can improve vocabulary.
  • Stereotypes: Media reinforces gender roles and beauty standards.

CTET Exam Tip: "School" is an agency of Formal and Secondary socialization. "Neighbourhood" is technically Secondary but acts informally like family. If asked, treat Neighbourhood as a secondary agent influencing daily interaction.

3. Anticipatory Socialization

CTET

Anticipatory Socialization is the process of "social rehearsal" where an individual adopts the values and behaviors of a group they aspire to join in the future.

  • Key Concept: Based on Reference Groups (Robert Merton). We compare ourselves to a group we look up to.

  • Examples:

  • A medical student acting like a doctor.

  • A child playing "House" (acting as parents).

  • An intern dressing like a full-time employee.

  • Function: It eases the transition into the new role and minimizes future "culture shock".

Contrast with Resocialization: Anticipatory adds new roles; Resocialization (e.g., prison/army) strips away old roles to build a radically new ones.

Deep Dive

Anticipatory socialization is a form of social rehearsal. It is the process where an individual learns the values and behaviors of a status or group they are not yet part of, but aspire to join in the future.

1. The Concept of Reference Groups

Sociologist Robert Merton introduced this concept. We always compare ourselves to a "Reference Group." If a village boy wants to become a corporate officer, he might start dressing, speaking, and eating like corporate employees long before he gets the job.

  • Function: It eases the transition into the new group. When the individual finally enters the group, the "culture shock" is minimized.

2. Key Examples

  • Career Preparation: Medical students wearing white coats and learning doctor-patient etiquette.
  • Marriage: Engaged couples observing married life or attending counseling to learn the roles of husband/wife.
  • Childhood Play: Girls playing with dolls (anticipating motherhood), boys construction games (anticipating provider roles) - Note: This often reinforces gender stereotypes.

Bonus: Resocialization

While Anticipatory adds new roles, Resocialization involves stripping away old ones. It is a radical change of personality.

  • Total Institutions (Erving Goffman): Places like prisons, army boot camps, or mental asylums where individuals are cut off from the wider society.
  • Process: The old self is "mortified" (head shaved, uniforms issued, names replaced by numbers) to build a new obedient self.

CTET Distinction:

Primary: Learning to be human (Child).

Secondary: Learning to be a citizen (Student/Adult).

Anticipatory: Learning to be what you want to become (Aspirant).

Resocialization: Unlearning the past to survive a new restrictive environment (Inmate/Soldier).

4. Agents of Socialization

CTET

Socialization happens through various agencies, broadly classified as Active and Passive.

| Type | Agent | Role & Impact | |

| Active Agency | Family, School, Peer Group | Two-way interaction. Builds cooperation and conflict resolution. | |

| Passive Agency | Library, Media | One-way interaction. Affects opinions and biases. | |

Deep Dive

Agencies can be categorized by the nature of interaction:

| Type | Agent | Role & Impact | |

| Active Agency | Family, School, Peer Group, Health Club | Direct interaction (Two-way). You influence them, they influence you. Builds cooperation and conflict resolution. | |

| Passive Agency | Public Library, Mass Media (TV, Books) | One-way interaction. You absorb information but cannot directly influence the source. Affects opinions and biases. | |

5. Gender as a Social Construct

CTET

A critical distinction in CDP is between Sex and Gender. This topic challenges biological determinism.

  • The Distinction:

  • Sex (Biological): Determined by biology/chromosomes (Male/Female). It is fixed and constant.

  • Gender (Social): Constructed by society (Masculine/Feminine). It is fluid and changes with time/culture.

  • Key Terms:

  • Gender Stereotype: Fixed ideas about roles (e.g., "Pink is for girls").

  • Gender Bias: Unfair treatment based on gender.

Exam Trick: If the question asks "Gender is a ?", the answer is "Social Construct". If it asks "Sex is a ?", the answer is "Biological Entity".

Deep Dive

"One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman." — Simone de Beauvoir.

This quote encapsulates the core of this topic. CTET strictly test the understanding that Sex is biological nature, while Gender is sociological nurture.

1. Sex vs. Gender (The Core Distinction)

| Feature | Sex (Jins/Ling) | Gender (Samajik Ling) | |

| Nature | Biological & Physiological. | Social & Cultural. | |

| Determination | By Birth/Chromosomes (XX/XY). | By Upbringing/Society. | |

| Changeability | Constant/Static. | Dynamic/Fluid (Changes with time/place). | |

| Categories | Male, Female, Intersex. | Masculine, Feminine, Transgender, Non-binary. | |

2. Key Terminology for Exam

  • Gender Stereotype: A generalized view or preconception about attributes/characteristics that are or ought to be possessed by women and men.

Example: "Boys don't cry" or "Math is for boys, Arts is for girls." Teachers must challenge these, not reinforce them.

  • Gender Bias: Inclination or prejudice for or against one gender.

Example: A teacher paying more attention to boys in a science class.

  • Gender Constancy (Kohlberg): The realization (around age 6-7) that one's sex is permanent and cannot change simply by changing clothes or hair.
  • Gender Parity: Numerical equality. If a class has 50 boys and 50 girls, it has gender parity.

Teacher's Correct Response:

If a boy wants to play with a doll: Allow it. Use it to break the stereotype that dolls are only for girls.

If a textbook shows mom cooking and dad reading paper: Discuss it critically with students. Ask, "Can dad cook too?"

Goal: Move towards Androgyny (possessing both high masculine and high feminine positive traits, like being strong AND caring).

6. Role of Play in Socialization

CTET

Play is a vital instrument of socialization, teaching rules, roles, and cooperation.

  • Solitary → Parallel → Cooperative Play progression.
Deep Dive

As per Parten's stages of play, socialization evolves through play:

  • Solitary Play: Playing alone (Infancy). Focus is on self.
  • Parallel Play: Playing side-by-side but not together (Toddlers). Awareness of others exists.
  • Cooperative Play: Playing together with rules and shared goals (Late Childhood). Highest form of social play. This builds teamwork and negotiation skills.
