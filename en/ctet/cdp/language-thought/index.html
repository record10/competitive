Language & Thought

Theories of Language Development

CTET

How language is acquired is a major debate. Major theories include Nativist (Nature), Behaviorist (Nurture), and Interactionist views.

  • Noam Chomsky (Nativist): Humans have an innate biological ability to learn language.

  • LAD (Language Acquisition Device): Hypothetical brain structure.

  • Universal Grammar: Inherited mental structure common to all languages.

  • B.F. Skinner (Behaviorist): Language is learned through environment (Nurture).

  • Mechanism: Operant Conditioning (Imitation, Reinforcement, Association).

  • Critique: Fails to explain novel sentence generation.

  • Lev Vygotsky (Interactionist): Language is a result of social interaction and culture. It is the primary tool for cognitive development.

Key Difference: Chomsky = Innate Hardware. Skinner = Learned Behavior. Vygotsky = Social Tool.

Deep Dive

How do children learn language? This is a central debate in linguistics and psychology. CTET frequently asks about the "LAD" and the difference between Piaget and Vygotsky's views.

1. Noam Chomsky (Nativist Thoery)

Chomsky is the most important figure here. He argued that humans are biologically "pre-wired" to learn language.

  • LAD (Language Acquisition Device): A hypothetical tool in the brain that allows children to learn grammar and syntax naturally.
  • Universal Grammar: All human languages share a common underlying structure.
  • Critical Period: Language learning is most effective in early childhood (up to puberty). After that, the LAD weakens.

2. B.F. Skinner (Behaviorist Theory)

Skinner believed language is learned like any other behavior: through Operant Conditioning.

  • Imitation: Children copy what they hear adults say.
  • Reinforcement: When a child says "Mama" and gets a smile/hug, they are reinforced to say it again.
  • Critique: This theory cannot explain how children produce sentences they have never heard before (Creativity).

3. Lev Vygotsky (Interactionist)

For Vygotsky, language is a social tool.

  • Language develops through Social Interaction.
  • Private Speech: Children talk to themselves to guide their actions. This is crucial for cognitive development (Self-regulation).

Relationship between Language & Thought

CTET

The relationship between language and thought helps distinguish between key psychologists. Who leads whom?

  • Jean Piaget: Thought precedes Language.

  • Language is just a reflection of underlying cognitive skills.

  • Egocentric Speech: Useless self-talk that disappears with maturity.

  • Lev Vygotsky: Language and Thought are initially separate.

  • Around age 3, they merge to form "Verbal Thought".

  • Private Speech: Vital for self-regulation and problem solving. Evolution: Social Speech → Private Speech → Inner Speech.

  • Benjamin Whorf (Linguistic Relativity): Language determines Thought. The structure of a language affects its speakers' world view or cognition.

Exam Tip: Piaget says Thought comes first. Whorf says Language comes first. Vygotsky says they run parallel then merge.

Deep Dive

This is the classic "Chicken or Egg" logical problem in psychology.

1. Jean Piaget's View

"Thought precedes Language." (विचार भाषा से पहले आता है).

  • Language is just a vehicle for expressing thought.
  • A child first forms a mental schema (concept of a 'ball') and then learns the word 'ball'.
  • Egocentric Speech: Piaget viewed children talking to themselves as "Egocentric" (useless/immature), which disappears as they socialize.

2. Lev Vygotsky's View

"Language and Thought are initially separate but merge around age 3."

  • At first, thought is non-verbal, and speech is pre-intellectual.
  • Around age 3, they merge to form Verbal Thought (Inner Speech).
  • Language is not just for expression; it shapes thinking.
  • Private Speech: Vygotsky viewed self-talk as vital for self-regulation and problem solving. It becomes "Inner Speech" in adults.

3. Sapir-Whorf Hypothesis (Linguistic Relativity)

"Language determines Thought." (भाषा विचार को निर्धारित करती है).

  • The language we speak dictates how we perceive the world.
  • Example: Eskimos have many words for 'Snow', so they perceive snow differently than people living in deserts.
  • Strong Version: Language determines thought (Rejected now).
  • Weak Version: Language influences thought (Accepted).

Stages of Language Development

CTET

Language acquisition occurs in a predictable universal sequence. It moves from simple sounds to complex sentences.

  • Pre-Linguistic (0-1 yr):

  • Crying: First sign.

  • Cooing (6 wks): Vowel sounds.

  • Babbling (6 mos): Consonant-Vowel chains ("ba-ba"). Universal.

  • Linguistic Stages:

  • Holophrastic (1 yr): One word = One sentence ("Water").

  • Telegraphic (1.5-2 yrs): Two words. Noun+Verb ("Mommy go"). No grammar.

  • Skills (LSRW): Listening & Reading are Receptive. Speaking & Writing are Productive/Expressive.

Syntax Rule: Children master semantics (meaning) before syntax (grammar).

Deep Dive

Language acquisition follows a universal sequence, regardless of culture.

1. Pre-Linguistic Stage (0-12 months)

  • Crying (Birth): The first form of communication.
  • Cooing (6 weeks): Vowel-like sounds ("oooo", "aaaa"). Shows comfort.
  • Babbling (6 months): Consonant-Vowel combinations ("ba-ba", "ma-ma"). This is universal even in deaf babies (though they stop later).

2. Linguistic Stage (1 year onwards)

  • Holophrastic Stage (1 year): One-word stage. A single word represents a whole sentence. (e.g., "Milk" means "I want milk").
  • Telegraphic Speech (18-24 months): Two-word stage. Noun + Verb ("Doggie bark", "Daddy go"). Grammar words (is, the, a) are omitted, like a telegram.
  • Complex Sentences (3 years+): Grammar rules appear. Over-regularization errors occur (e.g., "I goed" instead of "I went").

3. Language Skills

The four basic skills are LSRW. They should be taught in an integrated manner, not in isolation.

  • Listening (Receptive)
  • Speaking (Productive)
  • Reading (Receptive)
  • Writing (Productive)
