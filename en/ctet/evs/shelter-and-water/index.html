Shelter and Water

Shelter: Diversity in Houses <TopicTags tags={[EXAMS.CTET.PT, EXAMS.CTET.ET]} size="sm" />

CTET

1. Assam (Rain): Houses are built on strong bamboo pillars 10-12 feet (3-3.5m) above the ground to avoid floods. The interiors are also made of wood. They have slanted roofs to let heavy rain flow off. Bamboo is used because it does not rot easily in water. In flood-prone areas, this elevation is a life-saver. Molan Village in Assam is a prime example where such houses are commonly found.

2. Manali (Mountains): Houses are made of stone or wood. They also have slanted roofs due to heavy rain and snow in Himachal Pradesh. Unlike Assam, these are not on pillars. This construction style helps keep the house warm during freezing winters. Stone and wood are excellent natural insulators.

3. Ladakh (Cold Desert): 2-floor houses made of stones, coated with a thick layer of mud and lime. Ground floor: No windows, used for animals and storage. First floor: Family lives here. Roof: Flat and made of strong tree trunks. It is the most important part, used for drying red chillies, sitaphal, and paddy in summers. In winter, people move to the ground floor for warmth and to be close to their livestock. White lime helps reflect sunlight in summer to keep it cool.

4. Rajasthan (Hot Desert): Houses look like small forts and are made of mud. Walls are very thick to insulate against heat. The floor is plastered with cow dung to keep insects away. Roofs are made of thorny bushes (Keekar/Acacia) as wood is scarce. This keeps the house naturally cool even in scorching heat. Locals build these continuously using natural materials available in the arid landscape.

5. Kashmir: Houseboats (80ft long) have 'Khatamband' wood carving (jigsaw pattern). Donga are boat-houses for locals. Old houses have 'Dab' (protruding windows) with arched designs. Tourists flock from all over to experience a stay in these floating heritage homes. Sitting in the 'Dab' and watching the Jhelum river flow is a unique experience.

Deep Dive

A house is not just walls and a roof; it is a response to the climate and environment. In a vast country like India, housing styles vary dramatically from the wet hills of the North East to the dry deserts of the West.

1. Assam (Land of Heavy Rains)

  • Climate: Assam receives very heavy rainfall, and floods are common. Ground-level houses would easily get submerged.
  • Adaptation: To survive this, people build their houses on strong Bamboo Pillars. These stilts raise the house about 10 to 12 feet (3-3.5 meters) above the ground.
  • Construction: Bamboo is used because it is abundant in the region and doesn't rot easily in water. The houses are made of wood from the inside as well. The roofs are kept sloping so that the heavy rainwater runs off quickly and doesn't collect.

2. Manali vs Ladakh (Mountain Variations)

Both are in the mountains, but their designs differ:

| Feature | Manali (Himachal Pradesh) | Ladakh (Cold Desert) | |

| Structure | Made of Stone or Wood. | Made of Stones cut and kept one over the other. Coated with thick Mud and Lime. | |

| Roof | Sloping roofs. This is crucial because Manali gets rain and snow. Sloping roofs prevent water accumulation. | Flat roofs made of thick tree trunks. Rain is very less here. Roofs are used for drying Red chillies, Sitaphal, Maize, and Paddy. | |

| Floors | Usually single or double storey. | 2 floors. Ground floor has no windows. It is for animals and storage. In harsh winters, family also moves to ground floor for warmth. | |

3. Rajasthan (Hot Desert Adaptation)

  • Climate: Extremely hot with very little rainfall.
  • Structure: Houses are made of Mud. The walls are made very thick. Thick mud walls act as insulators—they don't let the outside heat enter inside, keeping the house cool naturally.
  • Plaster: The walls are plastered with mud. The floor is coated with Cow Dung mixed with mud. This traditional practice prevents insects from entering the house.
  • Roof: The roofs are made of Thorny bushes (Kikar, Acacia wood). Since wood is scarce, local bushes are used.

4. Kashmir (Architecture on Water)

Kashmir offers unique shelters that attract tourists from all over the world.

  • Houseboat: Found in Dal Lake and Jhelum river. They are huge—up to 80 feet long and 8-9 feet wide. Inside, there is beautiful wood carving on the ceiling called 'Khatamband', which looks like a jigsaw puzzle.
  • Donga: These are boats where local families live. They have different rooms like a normal house. You can see many Dongas lining the Dal Lake.
  • Local Houses: Old houses in Srinagar have a special type of window which comes out of the wall. This is called 'Dab'. It is beautiful to sit there and enjoy the view. The doors and windows have beautiful arches (Mehraab).

5. Disaster Management: Bhuj Earthquake

On 26th January 2001, a devastating earthquake hit Kutch, Gujarat. It destroyed thousands of stone and concrete houses.

  • The Experience: People were running in panic. "The ground shook like waves on water." Help arrived from all over (Army, foreign countries).
  • Rebuilding with Safety: Engineers and architects taught villagers to build safer houses. They suggested not to use heavy stones. Instead, they mixed clay with cow dung to make cakes (blocks), built walls, and whitewashed them. The roof was made of thatch and bamboo. Why? Because if a lightweight roof collapses during a quake, it won't kill people inside. This is "Disaster-Resistant Architecture".

The Changpa Community & Pashmina <TopicTags tags={[EXAMS.CTET.PT, EXAMS.CTET.ET]} size="sm" />

CTET

1. Changpa Tribe: A nomadic tribe living at 5000m (Changthang) in the mountains of Ladakh. They have only about 5000 people. They are always on the move with their goats and sheep. "Jule" means Welcome. "Changthang" means a place with very few people. Life is extremely tough at this altitude due to low oxygen levels. These people survive without any modern amenities in harsh conditions.

2. Rebo (Tent): They live in big cone-shaped tents called Rebo. It is made by weaving strips of Yak hair together. It is as strong as a wall and protects from icy winds (70 km/h). It is very warm inside. Near the Rebo, there is a place to keep animals called Lekha made of stone walls. The stone walls protect the livestock from straying or being attacked by wild animals. The Changpas can pack their entire household on horses and move to a new location in just 2.5 hours.

3. Pashmina Wool: Changpa goats (at 5000m, -40°C) grow a layer of fine hair (undercoat). This hair is Pashmina. It is so fine that 6 strands of it equals 1 human hair. It cannot be woven by machine. A weaver takes 250 hours to make one plain shawl. A Pashmina shawl is as warm as 6 sweaters but very thin and light. It is a symbol of prestige and craftsmanship. The entire economy of the Changpa people depends on this precious wool. Goats grow this hair in winter and shed it in summer.

Deep Dive

1. A Traveller's Tale (Gaurav Jani)

The NCERT chapter "A Shelter So High" narrates the journey of Gaurav Jani. He traveled from Mumbai to Delhi, and then to the high mountains of Ladakh on his motorcycle named 'Loner'. He carried a nylon tent, sleeping bag, stove, and camera. It took him 3 days to cover 1400 km from Mumbai to Delhi through Maharashtra, Gujarat, and Rajasthan. Finally, in Ladakh, he saw high, dry and flat lands called 'Cold Desert', which looked gray and barren but beautiful.

2. The Changpa Tribe

At a height of 5000 meters (Changthang), Gaurav met the Changpa tribe. It is a nomadic tribe living on the mountains. The tribe has very few people (only about 5000). They are always on the move with their goats and sheep.

  • Livestock is Life: Their goats are their only treasure. From them, they get milk, meat, skin for tents, and wool for coats/sweaters. A family's status is decided by the number of animals they own.
  • Living Conditions: It is difficult to breathe normally at 5000m due to low oxygen levels (headache and weakness is common for outsiders). There are no petrol pumps or mechanics. The Changpas pack their entire household on horses and yaks in just 2.5 hours and march to a new place!
  • Language: In their dialect, 'Changthang' means a place where very few people live. "Julie" is the magical word for Welcome.

3. Rebo: The Big Tent

The Changpas live in big cone-shaped tents called Rebo. It is not cheap plastic.

  • Construction: They weave strips from the hair of the Yak. Yak hair is very strong and warm. These strips are stitched together tightly. This fabric is incredibly strong and protects them from icy strong winds which blow at 70 km/hour.
  • Design: The tent is held up by two huge wooden poles. Inside, it is very spacious—you can stand straight. High up, there is an opening to let out the smoke from the chulha (stove).
  • Lekha: Near the Rebo, there is a place to keep sheep and goats called Lekha. The walls of a Lekha are made of stones. Families put a specific mark on their animals to identify them before taking them out for grazing.

4. The Treasure of Pashmina

These goats are special. They produce the world-famous Pashmina wool.

  • Survival of the Fittest: These goats live at high altitudes where the temperature drops to -40°C. To survive, nature gives them a coat of warm, soft hair (undercoat) which grows next to their skin. It is this fine hair which is Pashmina. In summer, they shed this hair.
  • Microscopic Fineness: This hair is so fine that 6 strands of it would be as thick as just 1 strand of your hair!
  • Processing: Because the hair is so fine, it cannot be woven by machines (machines would break it). It is hand-woven by skilled weavers in Kashmir.
  • Value: One Pashmina shawl is as warm as 6 sweaters! Yet it is very thin and light. It takes a weaver 250 hours (almost a month of work) to make one plain shawl. If it has embroidery, it takes much longer. It is a symbol of prestige.

Water: History & Science <TopicTags tags={[EXAMS.CTET.PT, EXAMS.CTET.ET]} size="sm" />

CTET

1. Historical Management: King Ghadsi of Jaisalmer built the Ghadsisar Lake 650 years ago. It was a group of 9 interconnected lakes; when one filled, water flowed into the next. This ensured water availability throughout the year. It was a community center with schools and ghats. Even today, we can learn from this example of how to manage rainwater harvesting effectively.

2. Al-Biruni: A traveler from Uzbekistan (1000+ years ago) who wrote about India's skill in building ponds (chabutaras with iron rods). His book provides historical evidence. He was fascinated by the intricate architecture surrounding the water bodies. His book was titled 'Kitab-ul-Hind', which remains a key historical source.

3. Bawri (Stepwells): Multi-storeyed wells where people went down steps to reach water instead of drawing it up. Used for community gatherings and resting (Adalaj Vav). Serving cold water to travelers was considered a pious act (Punya). In olden times, caravans of merchants would rest here during their journeys.

4. Science of Water:

  • Dead Sea: Saltiest sea (300g salt/litre). Humans float in it like wood because high salt increases water density. It is located between Jordan and Israel.

  • Float/Sink: A lemon sinks in plain water but floats in salty water (due to density).

  • Dandi March (1930): Gandhiji walked from Ahmedabad to Dandi to break the British salt tax law. Salt is made by evaporating sea water in shallow pits. Salt had a symbolic importance in the struggle for freedom.

5. Conservation: Tarun Bharat Sangh (TBS) helps build Johads. They helped 'Darki Mai' in Alwar (Rajasthan) to solve water shortage for her village animals. This revived the local ecology and economy. Rajendra Singh is known as the "Waterman of India" for his work with TBS.

Deep Dive

1. Ghadisisar Lake (A Miracle in the Desert)

In Jaisalmer (Rajasthan), where there is hardly any rain, King Ghadsi built the Ghadisisar Lake 650 years ago. It was an engineering marvel.

  • Interconnected System: It was not a single lake. It was a chain of 9 interconnected lakes built at different levels. When the first lake filled up with rain, the extra water flowed into the second lake, then the third, and so on. This ensured that no water was wasted, and the city had water for the entire year.
  • Community Space: Around the lake, there were pucca ghats, verandas, and rooms. People came here to celebrate festivals. Children came to study in the schools on the ghat.

2. Through the Eyes of Al-Biruni

A traveler named Al-Biruni came to India from Uzbekistan (more than 1000 years ago). He carefully observed and wrote about things he found unique.

"The people of India are very skilled at making ponds. They pile up huge rocks and join them with iron rods to build Chabutaras (raised platforms) all around the lake. My countrymen would be surprised to see them."

3. Stepwells (Bawri): A Lost Tradition

Before the arrival of modern pumps, communities built Stepwells. A stepwell is a deep well with steps leading down to the water level. Instead of pulling the water up with a rope (which is hard work), people could walk down to the water. Adalaj Vav near Ahmedabad is a classic example. These were not just water sources but resting places for caravans. Thirsty travelers were always given water (Piau) because serving water is a pious act.

4. Experiments: Float, Sink, and Dissolve

  • Lemon Magic: A lemon sinks in a glass of plain tap water. But if you dissolve plenty of salt in that water, the lemon starts floating! Why? Because salt makes the water 'thicker' (denser). The lemon is lighter than this thick salty water.
  • The Dead Sea: This is a sea where the water is dangerously salty. It has about 300 grams of salt in 1 liter of water. It is so dense that a person cannot drown in it. Even if you don't know how to swim, you will float on the surface as if lying on a bed!

5. Water Conservation Heroes

Tarun Bharat Sangh (TBS) is a real organization helping villagers.

  • Darki Mai's Story: She lived in a village in Alwar, Rajasthan. The village faced severe water scarcity. Women spent their entire day pulling water for animals. Darki Mai asked TBS for help.
  • The Solution: Together, they built a Johad (earthen check dam) to catch rainwater. The result was magical—the dried-up wells got recharged. The village which had no water now had enough for farming. Animals got green grass, and milk production went up.

6. Customs related to Water

In Uttarakhand, there is a custom where a newly wed bride goes and worships the village spring or pond. This shows respect for water. In cities, we see a modified version—the bride offers prayer to the water tap in her new home! This reminds us that water is life.

Birds and their Nests <TopicTags tags={[EXAMS.CTET.PT, EXAMS.CTET.ET]} size="sm" />

CTET

1. Unique Nests:

  • Crow: High on tree, uses wire/wood. Strong.

  • Koel: Lays eggs in Crow's nest. Never makes its own nest.

  • Tailor Bird: Uses its sharp beak to stitch two leaves on a bush like a needle.

  • Barbet (Coppersmith): Makes a deep hole in the tree trunk (tuk-tuk sound).

  • Weaver Bird (Baya): Male weaves beautiful hanging nests. Female inspects and chooses the best one to lay eggs.

  • Sunbird: Hangs from a small branch (hair, grass, cobwebs).

  • Dove: Among cactus thorns or mehendi hedge.

  • Indian Robin: Soft nest among stones on the ground (wool/hair).

Note: Birds use nests only to lay eggs and raise chicks, not to live in permanently. Once the chicks fly away, they leave the nest.

2. Beak Functions:

  • Hooked: To tear meat (Eagle, Vulture).

  • Curved: To crack hard nuts (Parrot).

  • Broad & Flat: To strain muddy water (Duck).

  • Long & Thin: To suck nectar (Hummingbird).

  • Chisel-like: To bore into wood (Woodpecker).

The shape of the beak depends entirely on the type of food the bird eats.

Deep Dive

Birds are the engineers of nature. They use their beaks as tools to build intricate homes. Remember, birds build nests only to lay eggs. Once the babies grow up and fly, they leave the nest forever.

1. Nesting Habits (Who builds what?)

The Crow

The crow is not picky. It builds its nest high up on a tree. It uses all kinds of materials—even hard things like pieces of wire and wood. It is a sturdy nest.

The Koel (Cuckoo)

The Koel is very clever (or lazy!). It sings beautifully but never makes its own nest. It lays its eggs in a Crow's nest. The crow, thinking they are its own eggs, hatches them. Sometimes the Koel even kicks out the Crow's eggs!

The Dove

The Dove makes a soft nest among the thorns of a Cactus plant or in a Mehendi hedge. This thorny location protects the edges from predators.

Sparrow & Pigeon

They live close to humans. A Sparrow builds a nest anywhere in the house—on top of a cupboard, behind a mirror, or on a ledge. A Pigeon often makes a nest in old deserted buildings or ventilators.

Coppersmith Barbet (Basant Gauri)

You can hear its 'Tuk-Tuk-Tuk' call in the summer. It uses its strong beak to make a hole deep in the tree trunk and lays eggs inside. It doesn't build an external nest.

The Tailor Bird

True to its name, it uses its sharp beak like a needle to stitch two broad leaves together on a bush. It stuffs the fold with soft cotton or wool to make a cozy cushion for eggs.

The Sunbird

It makes a delicate nest that hangs from the branch of a small tree or bush. The nest is a collection of hair, grass, dry leaves, cotton, and even spider webs.

The Weaver Bird (Baya)

The male weaver bird is the artist. He weaves beautifully hanging nests. The female weaver bird flies around, inspects all the nests, and chooses the strongest and best one to lay her eggs. She is the queen who chooses the palace!

Indian Robin

It makes a soft nest of grass, soft twigs, roots, wool, hair and cotton between stones on the ground.

2. Beaks: Tools for Survival

A bird's beak tells you what it eats.

  • Curved Beak (Parrot): Strong curve to crack open hard nuts and fruits.
  • Broad & Flat Beak (Duck): It has strainer holes on the sides. While eating muddy water, the mud and water flow out, keeping the insect/plant inside.
  • Hooked Beak (Eagle/Vulture): Very sharp hook to tear the flesh of prey.
  • Long/Thin Beak (Hummingbird/Sunbird): Like a straw, to suck nectar from flowers.
  • Chisel-shaped Beak (Woodpecker): Very strong to drill holes into hard wood to find insects.

3. Feet Claws

  • Webbed Feet: Skin between toes (Duck) acts like a paddle for swimming.
  • Long Slender Legs: (Crane, Flamingo) To wade in water without wetting feathers.
  • Sharp Talons: (Eagle, Hawk) Strong curved claws to grip and lift small animals (mice/snakes).
  • Slender Toes: (Sparrow) Three toes front, one back, to firmly grip (perch) on tree branches so they don't fall while sleeping.
